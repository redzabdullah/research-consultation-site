A first-time PhD student’s guide to data discovery

  1. 1

    Step 1: Start with the research question, not the database

    A clear question drives everything. Before touching any database, be clear on:

    • What phenomenon are you studying?
    • What is the unit of analysis?Firms? Individuals? Countries? Documents?
    • What variables do you need?
    • What time period?
    • What geography?
    • What methodology are you likely to use?Regression? Qualitative? NLP? Panel data?

    The database is the vehicle. Your question is the destination.

  3. 3

    Step 3: Conduct “data landscape scanning” early

    Do your reconnaissance before committing. Ask the right questions:

    • Does the data exist?
    • Is it complete? Longitudinal? Structured?
    • Is it exportable? Is API access available?
    • Are identifiers available?
    • What is the coverage (time and geography)?
    • Is there survivorship bias?
    • What does the missingness look like?
    • Is access restricted? Are there licensing limits?

    Exploration now prevents painful pivots later.

  4. 4

    Step 4: Know the difference: “interesting topic” vs “researchable topic”

    Interesting topic (too vague)

    “How AI changes employee happiness globally.”

    • How is “employee happiness” measured?
    • At what scale? Across countries?
    • Longitudinally? Is the data publicly available?

    Researchable topic (operationalisable)

    “Sentiment analysis of Glassdoor reviews among Fortune 500 firms before and after AI adoption announcements.”

    Make it measurable. Make it researchable.

    See it in practice Case 02

    Step 5: Learn identifiers early

    Identifiers are the keys that unlock and connect datasets.

    Why it matters

    • Real-world research requires merging data.
    • Merges fail without consistent identifiers.
    • Understanding identifiers saves time, reduces errors and improves data quality.

    Good researchers connect data well.

  6. 6

    Step 6: Understand the “grain” of the data

    Granularity (the level of detail) must line up across all your data.

    Examples of grain

    • DailyvsQuarterlyvsAnnual
    • Firm-levelvsSubsidiary-level
    • Article-levelvsCountry-level

    Mismatched grain = methodological problems.

    See it in practice Case 01

  7. 7

    Step 7: Expect data cleaning to take more time than analysis

    • 70% Cleaning
    • 20% Debugging
    • 10% Analysis

    Illustrative, a common rule of thumb.

    Plan your time realistically. Use reproducible workflows

    • Python / R / SQL
    • Jupyter / RStudio
    • Version control
    • Document everything

    Clean data is the foundation of credible research.

    See it in practice Case 01

  8. 8

    Step 8: Pilot first: never collect at full scale immediately

    Before going big, run a pilot. A pilot helps you validate:

    • Variable quality
    • Extraction logic
    • Feasibility
    • Computational cost
    • Methodological fit

    A small pilot today saves months (or years) later.

    See it in practice Case 05

  9. 9

    Step 9: Read methods sections aggressively

    Top papers are hidden tutorials. Reverse-engineer how the authors built their datasets. Ask:

    • Where did the authors obtain the data?
    • How did they merge it?
    • What filters did they apply?
    • What did they exclude?
    • What biases might remain?
    • Which identifiers did they use?

    Good methods reading = a faster learning curve.

The final principle

Think like a researcher, a data architect and a sceptic, not just someone who wants to download data.

Know what data should exist

Grounded in your question and theory.

Know what data actually exists

Understand its availability, quality and limitations.

Know whether it can validly answer your question

Ensure fit, validity and credibility.

That distinction separates a workable dissertation from a dead-end project.
